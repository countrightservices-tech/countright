Meals & Entertainment Rules

Resources • Tax Deductions • 11 min read

You just took a client to an amazing steakhouse. Great conversation, solid relationship building, and a $180 receipt. But is it deductible? The answer depends on rules that changed dramatically in 2018 — and many business owners still get them wrong.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) cracked down hard on entertainment while keeping most business meals alive at 50%. Add the temporary COVID-era 100% restaurant meal deduction that came and went, and it's no wonder people are confused.

This guide clarifies exactly what you can deduct in 2026, what's banned, and how to document everything so your deductions survive IRS scrutiny.

The Three Deduction Categories

Every business meal or entertainment expense falls into one of three buckets:

50%

Standard Business Meals

The most common category
  • Client/prospect dinners
  • Business travel meals
  • Coffee meetings
  • Team lunches (ordinary)
  • Meals during conferences
100%

Full Deduction Meals

Special exceptions
  • Company holiday parties
  • Company-wide picnics
  • Office snacks & coffee
  • Meals for promotional events open to public
  • Food sold to customers
0%

Banned Entertainment

NOT deductible at all
  • Sporting event tickets
  • Golf outings
  • Concert/theater tickets
  • Country club dues
  • Box seats / luxury suites
  • Hunting/fishing trips

What Happened: The TCJA Timeline

How the Rules Changed

Pre-2018
Entertainment was 50% deductible if business was discussed. Golf outings, box seats, and club memberships all qualified for a 50% write-off.
2018
The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act eliminated entertainment deductions entirely (0%). Meals remained 50% deductible. This was a major shift for relationship-driven businesses.
2021-22
COVID relief made restaurant meals 100% deductible to help the restaurant industry recover. This was always temporary.
2023+
The 100% restaurant provision expired. We're back to standard rules: business meals at 50%, entertainment at 0%, company events at 100%.

⚠ The #1 Audit Mistake: Coding entertainment as "Meals" to claim the 50% deduction. If the IRS sees the Ritz-Carlton golf resort or Live Nation on your statements categorized under meals, you're inviting trouble. Keep them in separate, clearly labeled categories.

💡 The "Who Was There" Rule: The strongest meal documentation answers four questions: Who attended? What was the business purpose? When and where did it occur? How much did it cost? Note these on every receipt or in your bookkeeping memo field.

How to Categorize Meals in Your Bookkeeping

Proper categorization is critical. Here's how to structure your chart of accounts for meals and entertainment:

Recommended Categories

  • Meals — 50% Deductible: Client meals, travel meals, team restaurant lunches
  • Meals — 100% (Company Events): Holiday parties, company picnics, staff appreciation events
    • Office Food & Refreshments: Coffee, snacks, water for the office
  • Non-Deductible Entertainment: Golf, event tickets, entertainment venues (keep separate for tracking even though 0%)

For a complete breakdown of ALL business expense categories, see our guide on how to categorize business expenses and our business expense checklist.

📊 Where the 50% Gets Applied: Don't multiply every meal by 50% in your bookkeeping software. Record the full amount in your meals category. Your CPA or tax software applies the 50% limitation when preparing Schedule C — this way your P&L shows true spending while your tax return reflects the deductible portion.

Common Questions About Specific Situations

Can I deduct my own meal if I'm traveling alone?

Yes, if you're on an overnight business trip (away from your tax home long enough to need sleep/rest), your meals — even eaten alone — are 50% deductible. Save the receipts and note the trip purpose.

What about food at a conference or seminar?

Meals included in your conference registration fee are generally treated as part of the education expense. Separately purchased meals during the conference are 50% deductible if the trip qualifies as business travel.

Can I bring my spouse's meal?

Generally no. Your spouse's meal isn't deductible unless they're a bona fide employee or business owner with a genuine business role at the meal. Family vacation meals combined with a business trip are also personal expenses.

What if the client pays for their own meal?

You can only deduct what YOU paid. If you each paid separately, there's no deduction for the other person's meal. If you paid the full bill for both, your portion AND their portion are deductible at 50% as a business meal (they were your guest for business purposes).
