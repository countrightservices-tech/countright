Vehicle & Mileage Deduction Guide

Resources • Tax Deductions • 12 min read

Your car is one of your business's biggest assets — and one of its biggest expenses. Whether you're a contractor driving between job sites, a consultant visiting clients, or an e-commerce seller making supply runs, the IRS lets you deduct the business use of your vehicle. But how you calculate that deduction makes a difference of thousands of dollars.

There are two methods: the standard mileage rate and the actual expense method. Choosing the wrong one — or failing to document your miles — can cost you serious money or trigger an IRS audit.

This guide breaks down both methods with real calculation examples, explains exactly what counts (and doesn't count) as business mileage, and shows you how to set up a bulletproof documentation system.

Method Showdown: Real Example

Let's compare both methods for a business owner who drove 10,000 business miles in a vehicle used 75% for business:

Standard Mileage

The Simple Method

Business miles driven 10,000
IRS rate (2024) 67¢
Your Deduction
$6,700
10,000 × $0.67

Just track miles  •  No receipts for gas/repairs  •  Covers all vehicle costs

Actual Expenses

The Detailed Method

Gas + repairs + insurance $6,000
Depreciation $3,500
Total vehicle costs $9,500
× Business-use % 75%
Your Deduction
$7,125
$9,500 × 0.75

Receipts required  •  More record-keeping  •  Potentially larger

💡 In This Example: The actual expense method produced a $425 larger deduction ($7,125 vs $6,700). For vehicles with high depreciation (new purchases) or heavy fuel costs, actual often wins. For fuel-efficient or older vehicles, standard mileage frequently comes out ahead — and is dramatically simpler.

What Counts as Business Miles (And What Doesn't)

The #1 mistake business owners make is counting commuting miles as business miles. Here's the breakdown:

Business vs Personal Miles

Home → Client Office

With a qualifying home office, this is business mileage from your principal workplace to a client location.

Deductible ✓
Office → Client Site → Office

Driving between business locations during the workday.

Deductible ✓
Office → Office Supply Store

Business errands like buying supplies, going to the bank, or picking up inventory.

Deductible ✓
Hotel → Conference Center (Business Trip)

Local driving during an overnight business trip.

Deductible ✓
Home → Regular Office (No Home Office)

This is commuting — never deductible, regardless of distance or traffic.

NOT Deductible ✗
Errands to Grocery Store / Personal Appointments

Personal errands are never business mileage, even if you stop on the way back from a client.

NOT Deductible ✗
Family Road Trip (Even If You Discuss Business)

Personal travel mixed with occasional business conversation doesn't qualify.

NOT Deductible ✗

⚠ The Commuting Rule: Driving from home to your regular workplace is never deductible. Business mileage starts after you reach your first business destination. If you have a qualifying home office as your principal place of business, ALL trips from that office to business locations become deductible — this is one of the biggest reasons to claim the home office deduction.

IRS Documentation Requirements

The IRS requires "adequate records" to support your vehicle deduction. Here's what you need:

Date & Destination

Every business trip needs a date and where you went. Mileage apps capture this automatically via GPS.

Business Purpose

Why was this trip for business? "Met with client ABC to discuss project" is sufficient. Note this at the time of the trip.

Odometer Readings

Record your starting odometer at the beginning of the year and ending at year-end. Apps or a simple note works.

Expense Receipts (Actual Method)

If using the actual expense method, keep receipts for gas, repairs, insurance, registration, and other vehicle costs.

📱 Recommended Apps: MileIQ, Everlance, Hurdlr, and QuickBooks' built-in mileage tracker all automatically detect trips via GPS, let you classify with one swipe, and generate IRS-ready reports. Most have free tiers for sole proprietors.

Vehicle Type Deduction Advantages

Different vehicle types have different deduction opportunities:

Standard Car / Sedan

Depreciation capped (~$20,200 year 1). Standard mileage often works best for fuel-efficient models.

Heavy Truck (>6,000 lbs)

May qualify for Section 179 & bonus depreciation — potentially deduct most of purchase price in year 1.

SUVs (6,000+ lbs)

Section 179 capped at ~$30,500 year 1, plus bonus depreciation on remainder.

Cargo Vans

If over 6,000 lbs GVWR and used >50% for business, full Section 179 available.

Motorcycles

Eligible for standard mileage rate if used for business, though special rules apply.

Leased Vehicles

Deduct business-use portion of lease payments. Watch for "inclusion amounts" on expensive leases.

How to Choose Your Method

Here's a quick framework for deciding between standard mileage and actual expenses:

Choose Standard Mileage If:

  • You want minimal record-keeping (just track miles)
  • You drive a fuel-efficient or hybrid vehicle
  • Your vehicle is older with low depreciation remaining
  • You have moderate annual mileage (under 15,000 business miles)
  • You're using a vehicle for business for the first year

Choose Actual Expenses If:

  • You recently purchased an expensive vehicle (large depreciation)
  • You drive a heavy SUV or truck over 6,000 lbs
  • You have very high business-use percentage (80%+)
  • Your vehicle has high fuel, insurance, or repair costs
  • You don't mind keeping detailed expense receipts

💡 Pro Tip — Run Both Calculations: There's no rule against calculating both methods and choosing whichever produces the larger deduction (you just can't claim both).
